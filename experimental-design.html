Experimental Design: The Foundation of Reliable RNA-seq

No amount of computational cleverness can rescue a poorly designed experiment. The decisions you make before sequencing — how many replicates, which controls, what sequencing depth — determine whether your differential expression results are trustworthy.

Biological vs. Technical Replicates

This is the single most important distinction in RNA-seq design.

Biological Replicates

Independent samples from different biological units: different mice, different patients, different cell culture passages, different fields in an agricultural trial. They capture biological variation — the variation you care about when generalizing to a population. These are required for statistical inference.

Technical Replicates

The same biological sample split and processed independently (e.g., same RNA library sequenced on two lanes). They capture technical variation from library prep and sequencing. These do not increase statistical power for biological conclusions. Pool technical replicates before analysis, or average them.

Rule of thumb: Budget for biological replicates first. A typical minimum is 3 biological replicates per condition; 5–6 is better for detecting modest fold changes. With fewer than 3, you cannot reliably estimate dispersion.

Choosing the Number of Replicates

The power to detect differential expression depends on:

  • Effect size: Larger fold changes need fewer replicates
  • Biological variability: High variability (e.g., human patients) needs more replicates than low variability (e.g., inbred mice)
  • Desired FDR and power: Standard is FDR ≤ 0.05, power ≥ 0.8
  • Sequencing depth: Deeper sequencing reduces sampling noise but not biological variance

Tools like RNASeqPower (R), PROPER, or Scotty can simulate power for your design. For a typical mammalian cell line experiment with moderate variability, 3–4 replicates per condition gives ~80% power to detect 2-fold changes at FDR 5%. For human tissue studies, plan for 6+ replicates.

Controls and Conditions

  • Untreated/vehicle control: Essential for any treatment experiment. Match the solvent (DMSO, ethanol) in treated samples.
  • Time course: Include multiple time points if the response is dynamic. Each time point needs its own replicates.
  • Dose response: At least 3 doses plus control, with replicates at each dose.
  • Batch-matched controls: If you must process samples in batches, distribute conditions evenly across batches.

Sequencing Depth

How many reads per sample? It depends on your goals:

ApplicationRecommended DepthNotes
Standard differential expression (mammalian)20–30 million paired-end readsSufficient for most protein-coding genes
Low-expression genes, non-coding RNA50–100 million readsDeeper sequencing captures rare transcripts
Alternative splicing analysis50–100 million paired-end readsLonger reads (150 bp) help resolve isoforms
Single-cell RNA-seq50,000–100,000 reads/cellDepends on cell type complexity
Bacterial/yeast (smaller genomes)10–20 million readsLess complexity, fewer reads needed

Paired-end reads (2 × 75 bp or 2 × 150 bp) are strongly recommended over single-end for splice-aware alignment and isoform resolution. The cost difference is modest; the information gain is substantial.

Blocking and Randomization

Batch effects are a major confounder in RNA-seq. Sources include:

  • Library preparation date/technician
  • Sequencing lane/run
  • RNA extraction date
  • Flow cell

Design strategy: Randomize samples across batches. If you have 6 control and 6 treated samples processed in 2 library prep batches, put 3 control + 3 treated in each batch. This lets you model batch as a covariate in DESeq2 (~ batch + condition).

If randomization is impossible (e.g., all controls processed before all treatments), you cannot computationally separate batch from condition — the design is confounded.

Strandedness

Stranded libraries preserve the strand of origin, which:

  • Resolves overlapping genes on opposite strands
  • Improves quantification accuracy for antisense transcripts
  • Is essential for certain library prep kits (dUTP-based)

Know your library prep protocol. Common types: forward-stranded (read 1 = sense strand), reverse-stranded (read 1 = antisense), unstranded. Tell your quantifier (featureCounts, Salmon) the correct setting.

RNA Quality: RIN and DV200

  • RIN (RNA Integrity Number): 1–10 scale from Bioanalyzer/TapeStation. Aim for ≥ 7 for standard RNA-seq; ≥ 8 for isoform-level work. Degraded RNA (RIN < 6) biases 3′ ends.
  • DV200: Percentage of fragments > 200 nt. Used for FFPE samples where RIN is unreliable. DV200 ≥ 30% is acceptable for 3′-tag sequencing (e.g., QuantSeq).

Always measure RNA quality before library prep. Document RIN/DV200 for every sample; include in metadata.

Common Pitfalls

  1. No biological replicates: "We only have one sample per condition, but we sequenced it deeply." — Cannot estimate biological variance; no p-values possible.
  2. Pseudoreplication: Treating technical replicates as biological replicates inflates false positives.
  3. Confounded batch and condition: All controls in batch 1, all treated in batch 2. Unfixable.
  4. Insufficient depth for the question: 10M reads for splicing analysis; 5M reads for low-abundance transcripts.
  5. Ignoring strandedness: Quantifying a reverse-stranded library as unstranded misassigns reads.
  6. Poor RNA quality not documented: Degraded samples distort expression profiles, especially 3′ bias.

Quick Design Checklist

  • [ ] ≥ 3 biological replicates per condition (5–6 preferred)
  • [ ] Technical replicates identified and will be pooled/averaged
  • [ ] Controls matched to treatments (vehicle, time-matched)
  • [ ] Samples randomized across library prep batches and sequencing lanes
  • [ ] Sequencing depth appropriate for the biological question
  • [ ] Paired-end reads (2 × 75 bp minimum, 2 × 150 bp preferred)
  • [ ] Library strandedness known and recorded
  • [ ] RNA quality (RIN/DV200) measured for every sample
  • [ ] Sample sheet with all metadata columns created
  • [ ] Power calculation performed (or justified by precedent)
